\title{OGC2026 Technical Report}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The OGC 2026 challenge combines bay assignment, two-dimensional placement,
orientation selection, and entry/exit scheduling for multi-layer polygonal
blocks. A feasible solution must satisfy both ordinary overlap constraints
and directional crane-access constraints. Our solver separates the problem
into an approximate scheduling stage and an exact geometric stage. It first
optimizes bay assignments and entry times under an area-capacity relaxation,
then converts the abstract schedule into a feasible placement using heuristic
precedence constraints and a crane-aware insertion routine. The resulting
solution is improved by parallel simulated annealing with large reconstruction,
shift, move, rotation, and swap neighborhoods. The main implementation
contribution is an event-based placement scanner that combines cached
relative-position collision grids with forbidden time intervals, allowing
position and entry time to be searched together. The solver also streams
intermediate candidates so that a validated solution remains available near
the time limit. Numerical aggregation of the training results is still
pending for this draft.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Challenge Problem \& Main Difficulties}

For each block, the solver must choose a bay, an allowed orientation, an
integer position, and entry and exit times. A block occupies its bay during
the half-open interval from entry to exit. Blocks present at the same time
must not overlap at equal layer heights. Moreover, an entry or exit operation
is feasible only if the block can be moved vertically without intersecting an
equal or higher layer of another block. This directional crane condition
makes feasibility depend not only on the set of simultaneous blocks, but also
on their temporal relationship and operation order.

The objective is
\begin{equation}
    w_1 Z_1 + w_2 Z_2 + w_3 Z_3,
\end{equation}
where $Z_1$ is total tardiness, $Z_2$ is the maximum normalized workload
imbalance among bays, and $Z_3$ is the total penalty for assigning blocks to
non-preferred bays. The weights differ by instance, so the solver must
balance schedule quality, workload distribution, and bay preference rather
than optimizing a single universal criterion.

The main challenge is the tight coupling between scheduling and geometry. A
good bay assignment may be difficult to realize geometrically, while a locally
compact placement can block a later crane operation. Exact collision checks
are also expensive because shapes can be non-convex, contain multiple layers,
and have several orientations. Since these checks occur inside a stochastic
local search, both the quality of the neighborhoods and the cost of evaluating
each candidate are critical.

\subsection{Contributions}

Our main contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A two-stage initial construction that first solves an area-relaxed
          bay/time problem and then reconstructs an exactly feasible geometric
          schedule.
    \item A crane-aware placement scanner that sweeps the $x$ coordinate and
          derives forbidden entry-time intervals for every equivalent range of
          positions.
    \item A large reconstruction neighborhood that removes related blocks in
          space and time, randomizes their priority model, and greedily
          reinserts them.
    \item A parallel simulated annealing framework combining large and small
          neighborhoods, shared-best exchange, and a tabu list.
    \item Lazy, shared collision preprocessing based on relative integer
          coordinates, together with streaming and validation of intermediate
          solutions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Algorithm Description}

\subsection{Overall Algorithm Framework}

The solver consists of preprocessing, abstract optimization, geometric
reconstruction, and two global improvement phases.

\paragraph{Geometric preprocessing.}
For every block orientation, we compute its bounding box and the range of
integer translations that fit in each bay. We also prepare the order of bays
by preference, the order of orientations by bounding-box area, similar
orientations for rotation moves, and pairs of blocks with comparable
footprints for swap moves. Expensive pairwise crane-collision data are not
fully materialized at this point; they are constructed lazily when requested.

\paragraph{Area-relaxed preoptimization.}
The first optimization stage ignores exact positions. For orientation $o$,
we define an approximate occupied area
\begin{equation}
 A_o = A^{\mathrm{union}}_o
       + \alpha\left(A^{\mathrm{bbox}}_o-A^{\mathrm{union}}_o\right)
       + \beta P_o,
\end{equation}
where $A^{\mathrm{union}}_o$ is the area of the union of all layers,
$A^{\mathrm{bbox}}_o$ is its bounding-box area, and $P_o$ is its perimeter.
For each block-bay pair, the minimum value over orientations that fit in the
bay is used. A bay is then modeled as a time-indexed area capacity.

The abstract state stores only a bay and an entry time for each block, with
exit fixed to entry plus processing time. Multiple randomized greedy builds
are attempted in parallel. The best one is improved by simulated annealing
using relocation, swap, and large reconstruction moves. The official
objective is evaluated exactly with respect to $Z_1$, $Z_2$, and $Z_3$ at this
level. During search, an additional congestion term penalizes simultaneous
use of a large fraction of a bay. This term is not included in the reported
objective; it encourages abstract schedules that are easier to realize
geometrically.

\paragraph{Geometric reconstruction.}
The abstract schedule supplies both bay assignments and temporal structure.
For blocks assigned to the same bay, we create a precedence relation when the
abstract intervals are separated by a configurable margin. These relations
form a directed acyclic graph. For each bay, the solver repeatedly generates
a randomized topological order and inserts blocks in that order. The priority
of a block combines normalized workload, footprint area times processing time,
preference spread, latest non-tardy entry time, slack, and a random term. The
best feasible construction for each bay is retained, and all bay schedules are
combined into the initial geometric solution.

\paragraph{Constrained and unconstrained improvement.}
The initial solution is first optimized while retaining the precedence graph.
This phase preserves the useful temporal structure found by preoptimization.
The constraints are then removed, and a second simulated annealing phase
searches the complete neighborhood. Separate temperature regimes are used
depending on whether the current solution has positive or zero tardiness.
Both phases are run by multiple workers from a common initial state. Workers
periodically import a sufficiently better shared solution, while maintaining
their own current state and local best.

\paragraph{Output and validation.}
The Rust solver emits improving candidates at bounded intervals. Each
schedule is converted into daily operations with all exits ordered before
entries. A Python wrapper keeps the candidates in objective order and checks
feasibility within the remaining time. This protects the final output from a
late failure or timeout in the stochastic search.

\subsection{Key Ideas that Succeeded}

The following ideas survived the development process and form the current
solver.

\paragraph{Joint placement and time scanning.}
The insertion routine fixes a bay, orientation, and $y$ coordinate, and then
sweeps $x$. For each existing block, relative-position collision intervals
produce events where the crane-interference state changes. Between two
consecutive events, every integer $x$ has the same set of forbidden temporal
relationships. The scanner therefore processes an entire $x$ range at once,
merges its active forbidden-time intervals, and returns the earliest feasible
entry time. This avoids enumerating every $(x,t)$ combination.

For a block $i$, the current solver always uses
\begin{equation}
    \mathit{EXIT}_i = \mathit{ENTRY}_i + P_i.
\end{equation}
An insertion candidate is ranked by its objective delta, entry time, and the
right and top edges of its bounding box. Bays are examined in preference
order, orientations in increasing bounding-box area, and $y$ coordinates in
random order. After a feasible $y$ is found, a small buffer of additional
values is examined. One of the top tied candidates is selected randomly to
preserve diversity.

\paragraph{Lazy directional collision grids.}
Each polygon layer is decomposed into convex parts. For a pair of convex
parts, a Minkowski difference gives the relative translations that cause an
intersection. These regions are rasterized into integer $x$ intervals for
each relative $y$. Layer pairs are combined according to the directional
crane rule, and both movement directions are generated together. The result
for a block-orientation pair is cached on first use and shared among all
workers. This representation turns repeated geometric queries in the local
search into interval lookups.

\paragraph{Structured large reconstruction.}
The principal diversification move removes a random number of blocks. Its
seeds are sampled from three complementary pools: blocks with a large current
penalty, blocks with high relocation freedom, and all blocks uniformly. The
remaining removed blocks are chosen near those seeds in a weighted distance
over $x$, $y$, and entry time, so that the move targets a coherent region of
the current space-time packing.

Removed blocks are reinserted in a newly generated priority order. The
weights of workload, geometric volume, preference spread, due-date urgency,
slack, current penalty, and randomness are sampled for every move. Thus the
same removed set can be rebuilt according to different notions of difficulty.
During reinsertion, the contribution of already fixed blocks to $Z_1$ and
$Z_3$ is a lower bound; the move is terminated early if that bound already
exceeds the simulated annealing acceptance threshold.

\paragraph{Local neighborhoods.}
Large reconstruction is combined with cheaper moves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Shift} changes $y$ while keeping bay and orientation fixed.
    \item \emph{Rotate} tries geometrically similar orientations near the
          current position.
    \item \emph{Move} selects a costly block from a pool of small-footprint
          blocks and reinserts it, possibly into another bay.
    \item \emph{Swap} exchanges the roles of two blocks with similar
          footprints and searches nearby alignments.
\end{itemize}
The mixture gives frequent inexpensive refinements while retaining a mechanism
for escaping local minima.

\paragraph{Parallel annealing with controlled information exchange.}
Four workers use independent PCG random streams. Each worker has a tabu list
over schedule hashes and records its own local best. A synchronized global
best is updated only by strict improvements. At configurable iteration
intervals, a worker imports it only when the score gap is sufficiently large,
which allows local trajectories to continue without completely isolating
them.

\subsection{Key Ideas that Failed}

Several explored mechanisms are not active in the current configuration.

\paragraph{Beam reconstruction.}
A beam-search version of large reconstruction was implemented. It precomputes
insertion candidates against the fixed part of the schedule and explores
combinations for the removed blocks. However, its candidate generation and
beam expansion are substantially more expensive than greedy reinsertion, and
the candidate set can become concentrated on a few bays or orientations. Its
current neighborhood weight is zero. We regard this method as deferred rather
than conclusively rejected; a fair quantitative comparison still needs to be
aggregated.

\paragraph{Reheating and heterogeneous worker temperatures.}
Reheating after stagnation and assigning different temperature multipliers to
workers were both investigated. Neither is active in the current parameter
set: the current implementation uses a geometric cooling schedule and equal
temperature scaling for all workers. These mechanisms introduced additional
tuning dimensions without a sufficiently stable benefit in the experiments
used during development. Their numerical comparison remains to be organized
before the final report.

\paragraph{More strongly decomposed searches.}
Bay-wise annealing and bay-wise preoptimization were introduced and later
removed. They simplify local geometry, but make cross-bay moves and the global
workload-imbalance term harder to optimize consistently. Earlier experiments
with kick moves, guided annealing, and ray-based insertion were also reverted
or removed in favor of the current, simpler combination of reconstruction and
scan-based insertion.

\paragraph{Explicit pair structures.}
We analyzed whether compact pairs of blocks, evaluated by their combined
convex hull, should be treated as temporary composite objects. Analysis code
was implemented, but pair structures are not used by the current solver. A
locally compact pair does not automatically improve the temporal crane
relationships, and maintaining composites would add another expensive layer
to every neighborhood.

\subsection{Further Improvement Plan}

The largest remaining modeling limitation is the fixed minimum residence time:
the solver always sets exit to entry plus processing time. The problem permits
a completed block to remain in a bay, which can change nested and sequential
interval patterns and enable better crane-operation orders. We plan to add
several entry candidates---the earliest feasible time, the original time, the
latest non-tardy time, and times immediately after forbidden intervals---and
to allow selected exit extensions.

Large reconstruction can also be made more targeted. Entry-time distance
will be replaced by the gap between residence intervals, and blocks that
directly prevent an urgent block from entering or exiting will be traced as a
blocker chain. The number of removed blocks and the amount of randomness can
then increase after stagnation. Target-bay and target-time-window seeds are
additional candidates.

For beam reconstruction, the main plan is to cache insertions against the
fixed base schedule and evaluate only interference with the few blocks added
inside the beam. Bay- and orientation-level quotas should preserve candidate
diversity. Finally, objective values, schedule hashes, and scanner workspaces
can be updated or reused incrementally rather than rebuilt for every move.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

The optimization core uses the 2024 edition of Rust. Its main libraries are
geo for polygon operations, rayon for shared-memory parallelism, and serde for
JSON and YAML input. PCG64Mcg provides reproducible random streams. All
algorithmic parameters are collected in a typed Rust structure and a YAML
file. This includes phase time budgets, cooling schedules, neighborhood
probabilities, removal distributions, reconstruction priorities, and scanner
settings.

The internal solution stores one record per block: block identifier, bay,
orientation, integer position, entry time, and exit time. Objective evaluation
computes total tardiness and preference penalties in one pass and accumulates
workload per bay for $Z_2$. Positive- and zero-tardiness states use separate
annealing temperature regimes because the relevant scale changes after all
due dates are met.

Collision preprocessing is optimized for frequent repeated queries. A
non-convex layer is triangulated when necessary, while a convex layer is kept
as one part. Pairwise directional grids are published through an atomic lazy
cache. The placement scanner reuses event arrays, counting-sort storage, and
compact slot sets for forbidden intervals. These choices avoid general
polygon intersection calls in the inner annealing loop.

Each global worker maintains its own state, local best, random generator, and
tabu queue. The shared-best object is protected by a short mutex section.
The implementation records, for every neighborhood, its selection count,
construction success rate, improvement rate, acceptance rate, objective
improvement, and CPU time. These diagnostics are intended for tuning the
mixture by improvement per unit time, not only by iteration count.

The submission wrapper launches the native solver as a subprocess and reads
streamed JSON candidates. Candidates are sorted by the solver's objective and
checked for feasibility before being returned. Phase limits are specified by
both a ratio of the total time and an absolute cap, and a final time buffer is
reserved for serialization and validation.

\section{Computational Results}

Evaluation uses the official three components: tardiness $Z_1$, normalized
workload imbalance $Z_2$, and preference penalty $Z_3$. We also separate
training instances with positive final tardiness from those where zero
tardiness is reached, because the dominant neighborhoods and appropriate
temperature scale differ between these groups. In addition to the weighted
objective, the final analysis will report feasibility and elapsed time.

For development comparisons, raw objective sums alone are potentially
misleading because the scale differs substantially among instances. We
therefore plan to report a per-instance relative score alongside component
values. The experiment tooling already records feasibility, objective,
elapsed time, and submission version, and can compare parameter variants on
fixed suites.
